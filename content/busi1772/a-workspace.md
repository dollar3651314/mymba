*Imagine that you are a strategy consultant hired by a large oil company to guide safety standards on oil rigs. Analyse in detail Milton Friedman's Shareholder theory and explain relevant differences with Edward Freeman's stakeholder theory. Then, explain which theory you would advise the oil company to follow, and why?*

**Introduction**

An oil company has asked me, as its strategy consultant, to help it stop serious accidents on its rigs from happening again. The core question is whether safety standards on oil rigs should only meet the legal minimum, or go beyond it.

The 2010 BP Deepwater Horizon disaster showed the dangers of meeting only the legal minimum. The explosion on the rig killed 11 workers (National Commission, 2011, p. vi). When managers make decisions to save money, regulation fails and there is strong pressure for profit, accidents become much more likely (National Commission, 2011, pp. vii, ix). Simply following the law is therefore not enough to protect workers.

This issue is important because the consequences affect many groups. For workers, lost lives cannot be brought back; for local communities, fishing, tourism and the environment suffer long-term damage that is hard to repair; for the company, BP's losses were about $65 billion (Bousso, 2018), and its reputation and share price were also damaged. No oil company wants to be the next BP.

My recommendation is that the company follows the ethical version of Freeman's stakeholder theory, because workers have a right to safety, and their safety is not just a cost. Friedman's shareholder theory places safety in the hands of the law, but laws and regulators can fail.

To support this argument, this essay has three parts. First, Section 1 explains the deontological basis of the two theories and shows their real difference: whether safety only needs to meet the law, or must protect workers' rights. Next, Section 2 responds to the strongest objections from Friedman and Heath and shows that they do not hold for oil rig safety. Finally, based on this analysis, the conclusion gives the company specific safety recommendations.

**1. Ethical Theory**

**1.1 Deontology**

Deontology is an ethical theory that focuses on rights and duties, rather than outcomes. Some actions cannot be done, no matter how good the outcome is, such as taking advantage of others or violating their rights. To treat people merely as a means is to use them only as tools to achieve a goal (for example, putting workers' lives at risk to save money), without respecting them as people with rights (BUSI 1772, 2026). Friedman and Freeman both make deontological arguments, but they differ on whose rights matter most.

**1.2 Friedman's Shareholder Theory**

In Friedman's view, a manager works for the owners of the company, the shareholders, as their agent. The manager's job is to run the company the way the owners want, which usually means to:

> make as much money as possible while conforming to their basic rules of the society, both those embodied in law and those embodied in ethical custom (Friedman, 1970, p. 211).

Therefore, Friedman is not saying that companies can ignore safety; he is saying that they should make as much money as possible within the law.

His reasoning has five steps (BUSI 1772, 2026):

1. Basic principle: no one may take away another person's property.
2. The owners decide: because the shareholders own the firm, they choose what it is for.
3. The owners' aim: in most cases, this aim is profit.
4. The manager's role: by contract, the manager works for the shareholders.
5. Conclusion: so the manager must make as much profit as possible.

Friedman gives an example: if a manager spends money to reduce pollution beyond what the law requires, he is spending the shareholders' money (Friedman, 1970, p. 212). Such spending is only justified when it serves the company's long-term interest (Friedman, 1970, p. 213). Safety measures on oil rigs that go beyond legal requirements are exactly this kind of spending.

**1.3 Friedman's Three Reasons**

Friedman argues that social problems should be left to the government, for three reasons:

- First, legitimacy: when a manager uses shareholders' money for social causes, it is like imposing taxes and deciding how to spend them without being elected (Friedman, 1970, p. 212).
- Second, competence: managers are experts in running a business, not in solving social problems: "How is he to know how to spend it?" (Friedman, 1970, p. 212).
- Third, motivation: managers who do this will be fired by the shareholders (Friedman, 1970, p. 213).

Therefore, according to Friedman's logic, safety standards on oil rigs should be set by the government through law, and companies only need to comply with them.

**1.4 Freeman's Stakeholder Theory**

For Freeman, managers answer to every stakeholder group – employees, customers, suppliers, communities and shareholders – because everyone has rights and no one should be treated merely as a means (Freeman, 1984, cited in BUSI 1772, 2026). His reasoning can also be divided into steps:

1. No one's rights may be violated.
2. Shareholders' property rights are only one kind of right; workers, customers and communities have rights too.
3. If these rights clash, someone has to weigh them against each other.
4. If the government does not do this, managers should.

Therefore, managers should make fair judgements between all parties, like King Solomon. On oil rigs, both workers and coastal communities are stakeholders, and they have a right to life and health, which cannot be traded for cost savings.

**1.5 The Key Difference**

Both theories require companies to comply with the law, so the real difference is not whether companies should be safe, but how far safety should go and why. Friedman believes that meeting legal requirements is enough, and that safety spending beyond the law is only justified when it brings profit. The ethical version of Freeman's theory holds that workers' safety is a right that must be protected even when it is not profitable. Table 1 summarises the main differences between the two theories.

Vogel points out that:

> many contemporary advocates of CSR have implicitly accepted Friedman's position that the primary responsibility of companies is to create wealth for their shareholders (Vogel, 2005, p. 26).

In other words, the strategic version of stakeholder theory, which cares about stakeholders because this makes money, is not very different from Friedman's view. Therefore, this essay adopts the ethical version of stakeholder theory. Whether the reason is "profit" or "rights" decides whether a company will continue a safety measure when it is "not cost-effective".

| | Friedman | Freeman (ethical version) |
|---|---|---|
| Managers are responsible to | Shareholders | All stakeholders |
| Source of the duty | Shareholders' property rights and the contract | Every person's rights |
| Who balances conflicting rights | Government | Managers, when government fails |
| How far safety should go | The legal minimum; more only if it brings profit | Protect workers' right to life, even if it is not profitable |

**2. Discussion**

**2.1 Why the Legal Minimum Is Not Enough**

Friedman argues that social problems such as inflation and unemployment should be left to the government (Friedman, 1970, p. 212). However, the risks on oil rigs are different: they come from the company's own business, and the first people to be harmed are the company's own workers. Therefore, workers' safety is not a "social problem" that can be passed on to the government, but the company's own responsibility.

Moreover, laws do not always protect workers. In the BP case, the regulator lacked staff and expertise, and many safety decisions were in fact made by the companies themselves. The US National Commission also concluded that regulation alone is not enough to ensure adequate safety, and that the industry must take its own steps to improve safety dramatically (National Commission, 2011, p. vii). If even the government's own investigation finds that the law is not enough, "following the law is enough" cannot be the safety standard for oil companies.

From a deontological perspective, making workers bear avoidable risks to their lives in order to save money is treating them merely as a means. A life cannot be brought back; money can be earned again, and no amount of money can buy a life.

**2.2 Objections from Friedman and Heath**

Friedman would argue that safety spending beyond legal requirements uses the shareholders' money, which the manager has no authority to spend; that managers cannot know how much safety is "enough"; and that when profits fall, the shareholders will replace the manager (Friedman, 1970, pp. 212–213).

Heath raises another problem: when managers are responsible to many stakeholders at the same time, their responsibility becomes unclear. Managers can use the excuse of acting "for employees" or "for shareholders" to do things that benefit themselves, and it is difficult to hold them accountable (Heath, 2014, cited in BUSI 1772, 2026). On an oil rig, a manager could use "safety" to justify any spending, or use "shareholder interests" to cut safety spending. This is the strongest objection to stakeholder theory.

**2.3 Responses**

However, these objections do not hold for oil rig safety.

- First, competence. Friedman's example is inflation, a macroeconomic problem in which managers are not experts (Friedman, 1970, pp. 212–213). Oil rig safety, by contrast, is an engineering and operational issue, which is exactly what oil companies specialise in; companies understand the risks on their own rigs better than governments do.
- Second, legitimacy. If the company sets its safety rules according to international and industry standards, and lets workers and communities take part in decisions, safety standards are no longer decided by the manager alone. According to Vogel (2005, pp. 166–167), governments and bodies like the ILO, the OECD and the United Nations helped to write many of these voluntary codes.
- Third, ambiguity. The company can write safety rules that are clear and can be checked: any worker who sees danger has the right to stop work; important safety decisions must be confirmed by two people (the "four-eyes principle"); and who has the authority to make decisions is decided in advance. The clearer the rules, the harder it is for managers to exploit loopholes, and the easier it is to hold them accountable.

Of the three responses, competence is the most persuasive. Expert work should be left to experts: the manager is appointed to run the drilling business, and safety is part of that business; the company has engineers and safety experts who understand the risks on its rigs better than the government does.

**2.4 Does Safety Always Pay?**

Some may argue that high safety standards always pay in the long run, so under Friedman's theory companies would also take safety seriously. However, Vogel finds no evidence that companies which behave more virtuously are more profitable (Vogel, 2005, p. 17), because not enough consumers and investors are willing to pay for virtue: "The market for virtue does not clear" (Vogel, 2005, p. 165). In other words, some safety measures do not make money.

Although safety measures do not always make money, companies pay huge costs when accidents happen, such as BP's losses of about $65 billion (Bousso, 2018) and the damage to its share price and reputation. From this perspective, safety can also be seen as a way of "making money". However, this is only an additional reason: when companies calculate costs and benefits before an accident, saving money often seems more cost-effective, as it did at BP. Therefore, safety must ultimately rest on rights, not on calculation.

In addition, Vogel suggests that companies should support raising legal standards (Vogel, 2005, p. 171). If the whole industry must follow the same safety standards, no company will be at a disadvantage because it takes safety seriously, and Friedman's motivation problem also becomes smaller.

**3. Conclusion and Recommendations**

Overall, the ethical version of stakeholder theory is the better guide for this company's rig safety. Laws and regulators can fail, and high safety standards do not always bring profit; only when safety is treated as workers' right will the company continue safety measures that are not cost-effective.

This essay therefore makes three recommendations. First, safety standards should go beyond the legal minimum and follow international and industry standards. Second, any worker who sees danger should have the right to stop work, and important safety decisions should be confirmed by two people. Third, the company should publicly support stricter legal safety standards for the whole industry.

These recommendations will increase costs, may reduce short-term profits and are hard for one company to sustain alone. However, compared with the loss of human lives and BP's losses of about $65 billion, these costs are worth paying.

**2. Discussion**

**2.1 Why the Legal Minimum Is Not Enough（为什么只守法不够）**
Friedman 认为社会问题，例如通胀和贫困，应该交给政府 (Friedman, 1970, p. 212)。但钻井平台的风险不同：它来自公司自己的业务，第一个受到伤害的是公司自己的工人。因此，工人的安全不是一个可以推给政府的“社会问题”，而是公司自己的责任。
其次，法律并不总能保护工人。在 BP 案中，监管机构缺少人手和专业知识，很多安全决定实际上由公司自己作出。美国调查委员会也认为，光靠监管不足以保证足够的安全，行业必须自己主动大幅提高安全标准 (National Commission, 2011, p. vii)。如果连政府的调查都认为法律不够，“守法就够了”就不能成为石油公司的安全标准。
从义务论看，为了省钱让工人承担本可以避免的生命危险，就是把工人仅仅当作手段。生命无法挽回，金钱可以再赚，再多的钱也无法购买生命。

**2.2 Objections from Friedman and Heath（来自 Friedman 和 Heath 的反对）**
Friedman 会反驳说：超出法律要求的安全开支是在花股东的钱，经理没有得到授权；经理也无法判断安全做到什么程度才算“够”；而且利润下降时，股东会撤换他 (Friedman, 1970, pp. 212–213)。
Heath 提出了另一个问题：经理同时对许多利益相关者负责，责任就变得模糊。经理可以拿“为了员工”或“为了股东”当借口，做对自己有利的事，而且很难被追究 (Heath, 2014, cited in BUSI 1772 Week 2 lecture slides)。在钻井平台上，经理既可以用“安全”来为任何开支辩护，也可以用“股东利益”来削减安全开支。这是对利益相关者理论最有力的批评。

**2.3 Responses（回应）**
但这些反对在钻井安全上并不成立。
第一，能力。Friedman 举的例子是通胀，这是经理不懂的宏观经济问题 (Friedman, 1970, pp. 212–213)。但钻井安全是工程和运营问题，正是石油公司的专业；公司比政府更了解自己平台的风险。
第二，合法性。如果公司按照国际和行业标准制定安全规则，并让工人和社区参与决定，安全标准就不是经理一个人说了算。Vogel 指出，很多自愿准则本身就是政府和国际组织（例如 ILO、OECD 和联合国）参与制定的 (Vogel, 2005, pp. 166–167)。
第三，模糊性。公司可以把安全写成清楚、可以检查的规则：任何工人发现危险都有权叫停作业；重要的安全决定必须由两个人确认（“四眼原则”）；事先规定谁有权作决定。规则越清楚，经理越难钻空子，也越容易被追究责任。
三条回应中，能力这一条最有说服力。专业的人做专业的事：经理被委任来经营钻井业务，而安全本身就是经营的一部分；公司有工程师和安全专家，比政府更了解自己平台的风险。

**2.4 Does Safety Always Pay?（安全一定赚钱吗？）**
有人会说，高安全标准长期来看总会赚钱，所以按 Friedman 的理论，公司也会重视安全。但 Vogel 发现，没有证据表明更讲美德的公司更赚钱 (Vogel, 2005, p. 17)；愿意为美德付钱的消费者和投资者不够多："The market for virtue does not clear" (Vogel, 2005, p. 165)。也就是说，有些安全措施确实不赚钱。
虽然安全措施不一定赚钱，但发生事故时，企业会付出巨额代价，比如 BP 约 650 亿美元的损失 (Bousso, 2018)，以及股价和声誉受到的影响。从这个角度看，安全也是另一种意义的“赚钱”。不过这只是补充理由：事故发生之前，公司算账时往往会觉得省钱更划算，BP 正是这样。所以安全最终要靠权利，而不是靠算账。
此外，Vogel 建议企业支持提高法定标准 (Vogel, 2005, p. 171)。如果全行业都必须遵守同样的安全标准，就没有哪家公司因为重视安全而吃亏，Friedman 说的“动力”问题也会变小。

**1. Ethical Theory**

**1.1 Deontology（义务论）**
义务论是看权利和义务的一种论证，而不是看结果。有些事不管结果多好都不能做——比如利用别人、伤害别人的权利。“仅仅当作手段”就是只把人当成达到目的的工具（比如为了省钱让工人冒生命危险），不尊重他也是一个有权利的人（BUSI 1772 Week 2 lecture slides）。Friedman 和 Freeman 都是义务论，只是对“谁的权利最重要”的看法不同。

**1.2 Friedman's Shareholder Theory（Friedman 的股东理论）**
Friedman 认为经理是股东（企业所有者）的雇员 / 代理人，责任是按股东的意愿经营企业，通常就是 "make as much money as possible while conforming to their basic rules of the society, both those embodied in law and those embodied in ethical custom" (Friedman, 1970, p. 211)。所以 Friedman 不是说“可以不顾安全”，而是“守法之内尽量赚钱”。
他的推理分五步（BUSI 1772 Week 2 lecture slides）：底层基础——侵犯别人的财产权是不允许的；关键决策人的权利——股东拥有公司，有权决定公司的目的；关键决策人的目标——股东的目的通常是利润；执行者——经理签了合同，代表股东；结论——经理有义务让利润最大化。
Friedman 举了一个例子：如果经理花钱减少污染，超过了法律的要求，他就是在花股东的钱 (Friedman, 1970, p. 212)。这样的开支只有在对公司长期有利时才是合理的 (Friedman, 1970, p. 213)。钻井平台上超出法律要求的安全措施，正是这种情况。

**1.3 Friedman's Three Reasons（Friedman 的三条理由）**
Friedman 认为社会问题应该交给政府，理由有三条。第一，合法性：经理拿股东的钱做公益，等于没经过选举就“征税”和花税款 (Friedman, 1970, p. 212)。第二，能力：经理是经营专家，不是解决社会问题的专家——“他怎么知道该怎么花？” (Friedman, 1970, p. 212)。第三，动力：这样做的经理会被股东炒掉 (Friedman, 1970, p. 213)。所以按 Friedman 的逻辑，钻井安全标准应该由政府通过法律来定，公司遵守就够了。

**1.4 Freeman's Stakeholder Theory（Freeman 的利益相关者理论）**
Freeman 认为，经理要对所有利益相关者负责，包括员工、顾客、供应商、社区和股东，因为每个人都有各自的权利，谁都不能只被当作手段 (Freeman, 1984, cited in BUSI 1772 Week 2 lecture slides)。他的推理也可以分几步：侵犯任何人的权利都是不允许的；股东有财产权，但其他利益相关者也有权利；各方权利冲突时，需要有人来平衡；政府没有做到时，经理应该来平衡。所以经理要像所罗门王一样，在各方之间作出公正的判断。在钻井平台上，工人和沿海社区都是利益相关者，他们的生命和健康是权利，不能被当作省钱的手段。

**1.5 The Key Difference（两个理论的关键区别）**
两个理论都要求公司遵守法律，所以真正的区别不是“要不要安全”，而是安全要做到什么程度、理由是什么。Friedman 认为做到法律要求就够了，超出法律的安全开支只有在能带来利润时才合理；Freeman 的伦理版认为，工人的安全是权利，即使不赚钱也要保护。Table 1 总结了两者的主要区别。

**1. Ethical Theory**（第 1 节标题）

**1a 义务论（约 120 词）**
本文用义务论来分析两个理论。义务论判断一个行为对不对，看的是它是否尊重人的权利、是否履行了义务，而不是只看结果好不好。它的核心要求是：不能把人仅仅当作达到目的的手段（BUSI 1772 第 2 周课件）。Friedman 的股东理论和 Freeman 的利益相关者理论都是义务论的论证：两者都认为经理必须尊重权利。它们的分歧在于：**谁的权利优先，以及由谁来保护其他人的权利。**

**1b Friedman 的股东理论（约 250 词）**
Friedman 认为 "Only people have responsibilities"（Friedman, 1970, p. 211），所以要问的是公司经理的责任。他认为，经理是企业所有者的雇员，责任是按股东的意愿经营公司，也就是 "make as much money as possible while conforming to their basic rules of the society, both those embodied in law and those embodied in ethical custom"（Friedman, 1970, p. 211）。
他的论证可以分四步：第一，侵犯别人的财产权是不允许的；第二，股东拥有公司，有权决定公司的目的；第三，股东的目的通常是利润；第四，经理通过合同同意代表股东。所以，经理有义务让利润最大化（第 2 周课件）。
需要注意的是，Friedman 并不允许公司违法或欺骗，所以他不是说公司可以不顾安全。但是，如果经理花钱做**超出法律要求**的事（例如减少污染），就是在花股东的钱（Friedman, 1970, p. 212）。只有当这笔开支符合公司的**长期利益**时，它才是合理的（Friedman, 1970, p. 213）。

**1c Friedman 的三条理由（约 150 词）**
Friedman 认为社会问题应该由政府解决，而不是由经理解决，理由有三条。第一，**合法性**：经理拿股东的钱做社会项目，等于一边“征税”、一边决定税款怎么花，但他不是选民选出来的（Friedman, 1970, p. 212）。第二，**能力**：经理是经营公司的专家，但不一定知道怎样解决社会问题（Friedman, 1970, p. 212）。第三，**动力**：牺牲利润的经理会被股东撤换（Friedman, 1970, p. 213）。按照这个逻辑，钻井安全标准应该由政府通过法律来定，公司只要遵守就够了。

**1d Freeman 的利益相关者理论（约 180 词）**
Freeman 认为，经理不只对股东负责，还要对所有利益相关者负责，包括员工、顾客、供应商、当地社区和股东（Freeman, 1984，引自第 2 周课件）。他的论证也分四步：第一，侵犯任何人的权利都是不允许的；第二，股东有财产权，但其他利益相关者也有权利；第三，当这些权利发生冲突时，需要有人来平衡；第四，当政府没有做到时，经理应该来平衡。所以，经理有义务平衡所有利益相关者的利益，像所罗门王一样作出公正的判断（第 2 周课件）。对钻井平台来说，工人和沿海社区都是利益相关者，他们的生命和健康是权利，不能被当作降低成本的手段。

**1e 两个理论的区别（约 150 词 + 一张对比表，表格不算字数——今晚问老师确认）**
两个理论都要求公司遵守法律，所以真正的区别不是“要不要安全”，而是**安全要做到什么程度、理由是什么**。Friedman 认为做到法律要求就够了，超出法律的安全开支只有在能带来利润时才合理；Freeman 的伦理版认为，工人的安全是权利，即使不赚钱也要保护。

**2. Discussion**

**2.1 Why the Legal Minimum Is Not Enough**

Friedman argues that social problems such as inflation and poverty should be left to the government (Friedman, 1970, p. 212). However, the risks on oil rigs are different: they come from the company's own business, and the first people to be harmed are the company's own workers. Therefore, workers' safety is not a "social problem" that can be passed on to the government, but the company's own responsibility.

Moreover, laws do not always protect workers. In the BP case, the regulator lacked staff and expertise, and many safety decisions were in fact made by the companies themselves. The US National Commission also concluded that regulation alone is not enough to ensure adequate safety, and that the industry must take its own steps to improve safety dramatically (National Commission, 2011, p. vii). If even the government's own investigation finds that the law is not enough, "following the law is enough" cannot be the safety standard for oil companies.

From a deontological perspective, making workers bear avoidable risks to their lives in order to save money is treating them merely as a means. A life cannot be brought back; money can be earned again, and no amount of money can buy a life.

**2.2 Objections from Friedman and Heath**

Friedman would argue that safety spending beyond legal requirements uses the shareholders' money, which the manager has no authority to spend; that managers cannot know how much safety is "enough"; and that when profits fall, the shareholders will replace the manager (Friedman, 1970, pp. 212–213).

Heath raises another problem: when managers are responsible to many stakeholders at the same time, their responsibility becomes unclear. Managers can use the excuse of acting "for employees" or "for shareholders" to do things that benefit themselves, and it is difficult to hold them accountable (Heath, 2014, cited in BUSI 1772 Week 2 lecture slides). On an oil rig, a manager could use "safety" to justify any spending, or use "shareholder interests" to cut safety spending. This is the strongest objection to stakeholder theory.

**2.3 Responses**

However, these objections do not hold for oil rig safety.

- First, competence. Friedman's example is inflation, a macroeconomic problem in which managers are not experts (Friedman, 1970, pp. 212–213). Oil rig safety, by contrast, is an engineering and operational issue, which is exactly what oil companies specialise in; companies understand the risks on their own rigs better than governments do.
- Second, legitimacy. If the company sets its safety rules according to international and industry standards, and lets workers and communities take part in decisions, safety standards are no longer decided by the manager alone. According to Vogel (2005, pp. 166–167), governments and bodies like the ILO, the OECD and the United Nations helped to write many of these voluntary codes.
- Third, ambiguity. The company can write safety rules that are clear and can be checked: any worker who sees danger has the right to stop work; important safety decisions must be confirmed by two people (the "four-eyes principle"); and who has the authority to make decisions is decided in advance. The clearer the rules, the harder it is for managers to exploit loopholes, and the easier it is to hold them accountable.

Of the three responses, competence is the most persuasive. Expert work should be left to experts: the manager is appointed to run the drilling business, and safety is part of that business; the company has engineers and safety experts who understand the risks on its rigs better than the government does.

**2.4 Does Safety Always Pay?**

Some may argue that high safety standards always pay in the long run, so under Friedman's theory companies would also take safety seriously. However, Vogel finds no evidence that companies which behave more virtuously are more profitable (Vogel, 2005, p. 17), because not enough consumers and investors are willing to pay for virtue: "The market for virtue does not clear" (Vogel, 2005, p. 165). In other words, some safety measures do not make money.

Although safety measures do not always make money, companies pay huge costs when accidents happen, such as BP's losses of about $65 billion (Bousso, 2018) and the damage to its share price and reputation. From this perspective, safety can also be seen as a way of "making money". However, this is only an additional reason: when companies calculate costs and benefits before an accident, saving money often seems more cost-effective, as it did at BP. Therefore, safety must ultimately rest on rights, not on calculation.

In addition, Vogel suggests that companies should support raising legal standards (Vogel, 2005, p. 171). If the whole industry must follow the same safety standards, no company will be at a disadvantage because it takes safety seriously, and Friedman's motivation problem also becomes smaller.

**1. Ethical Theory**

**1.1 Deontology**

Deontology is an ethical theory that focuses on rights and duties, rather than outcomes. Some actions cannot be done, no matter how good the outcome is, such as taking advantage of others or violating their rights. Treating people merely as a means means using them only as tools to achieve a goal (for example, putting workers' lives at risk to save money), without respecting them as people with rights (BUSI 1772 Week 2 lecture slides). Friedman and Freeman both make deontological arguments, but they differ on whose rights matter most.

**1.2 Friedman's Shareholder Theory**

Friedman argues that managers are employees, or agents, of the shareholders (the owners of the business). Their responsibility is to run the business according to the shareholders' wishes, which usually means to "make as much money as possible while conforming to their basic rules of the society, both those embodied in law and those embodied in ethical custom" (Friedman, 1970, p. 211). Therefore, Friedman is not saying that companies can ignore safety; he is saying that they should make as much money as possible within the law.

His reasoning has five steps (BUSI 1772 Week 2 lecture slides):

1. Basic principle: no one may take away another person's property.
2. The owners decide: because the shareholders own the firm, they choose what it is for.
3. The owners' aim: in most cases, this aim is profit.
4. The manager's role: by contract, the manager works for the shareholders.
5. Conclusion: so the manager must make as much profit as possible.

Friedman gives an example: if a manager spends money to reduce pollution beyond what the law requires, he is spending the shareholders' money (Friedman, 1970, p. 212). Such spending is only justified when it serves the company's long-term interest (Friedman, 1970, p. 213). Safety measures on oil rigs that go beyond legal requirements are exactly this kind of spending.

**1.3 Friedman's Three Reasons**

Friedman argues that social problems should be left to the government, for three reasons:

- First, legitimacy: when a manager uses shareholders' money for social causes, it is like imposing taxes and deciding how to spend them without being elected (Friedman, 1970, p. 212).
- Second, competence: managers are experts in running a business, not in solving social problems: "How is he to know how to spend it?" (Friedman, 1970, p. 212).
- Third, motivation: managers who do this will be fired by the shareholders (Friedman, 1970, p. 213).

Therefore, according to Friedman's logic, safety standards on oil rigs should be set by the government through law, and companies only need to comply with them.

**1.4 Freeman's Stakeholder Theory**

For Freeman, managers answer to every stakeholder group – employees, customers, suppliers, communities and shareholders – because everyone has rights and no one should be treated merely as a means (Freeman, 1984, cited in BUSI 1772 Week 2 lecture slides). His reasoning can also be divided into steps:

1. No one's rights may be violated.
2. Shareholders' property rights are only one kind of right; workers, customers and communities have rights too.
3. If these rights clash, someone has to weigh them against each other.
4. If the government does not do this, managers should.

Therefore, managers should make fair judgements between all parties, like King Solomon. On oil rigs, both workers and coastal communities are stakeholders, and they have a right to life and health, which cannot be traded for cost savings.

**1.5 The Key Difference**

Both theories require companies to comply with the law, so the real difference is not whether companies should be safe, but how far safety should go and why. Friedman believes that meeting legal requirements is enough, and that safety spending beyond the law is only justified when it brings profit. The ethical version of Freeman's theory holds that workers' safety is a right that must be protected even when it is not profitable. Table 1 summarises the main differences between the two theories.

Vogel points out that "many contemporary advocates of CSR have implicitly accepted Friedman's position that the primary responsibility of companies is to create wealth for their shareholders" (Vogel, 2005, p. 26). In other words, the strategic version of stakeholder theory, which cares about stakeholders because this makes money, is not very different from Friedman's view. Therefore, this essay adopts the ethical version of stakeholder theory. Whether the reason is "profit" or "rights" decides whether a company will continue a safety measure when it is "not cost-effective".

### A. 这道题到底问什么（一句话）
你是石油公司请来的顾问，管**钻井平台的安全标准**。要做三件事：① 详细讲 Friedman 的股东理论 ② 讲它和 Freeman 利益相关者理论的区别 ③ **告诉公司该用哪个理论、为什么**。

### B. 两个理论一句话
- **Friedman（股东理论）**：公司属于股东，经理是股东的雇员 → 在守法和遵守道德习俗的前提下**尽量多赚钱**。社会问题是**政府**的事；经理拿股东的钱做“社会责任”= 没有选举就收税。超出法律的安全开支，只有对公司**长期有利**时才做。
- **Freeman（利益相关者理论）**：员工、顾客、供应商、社区、股东**都有权利**，谁都不能被当作手段 → 权利冲突、政府又没管好时，**经理要来平衡**。工人的生命安全是权利，**即使不赚钱也要保护**。
- **两者真正的分歧**：不是“要不要安全”（两个都要求守法），而是 **“做到法律最低要求就够，还是要更高？理由是利润还是权利？”**

### C. 课上讲得最多的案例：BP 深水地平线（2010）
- 墨西哥湾钻井平台爆炸：**11 人死亡**，约 **490 万桶**原油泄漏，BP 总损失约 **650 亿美元**。
- 原因：为赶工期省钱、安全措施不够、政府监管弱、过度依赖行业自律。
- 老师的话：“**有法规是一回事，执法是另一回事。**”纪录片的问题：“**到底谁说了算？**”

## 📖 第 1 题回顾：血汗工厂（第 1 周）
**题目**：Analyse in detail Matt Zwolinski's argument in favour of sweatshop labour. Then, explain whether you agree with him or not, and why?
（详细分析 Zwolinski 支持血汗工厂劳动的论证，然后说明你是否同意他，为什么。）
**必读** Zwolinski (2007)；**推荐** Kates (2015)、Vogel (2005) 第 4 章。
**必须写到**：义务论 → Zwolinski 的论证 → 用推荐阅读讨论反对意见 → 同意 / 不同意。

**1. 义务论三条规则**：① 每个人有自主决定的空间；② 别人的权利约束你——不能把人“仅仅当作手段”，不能剥削；③ 有限的援助义务。
**剥削**：A 利用 B 的弱势获取不公平的好处——要同时证明：A 得到好处、好处不公平、B 只因弱势才接受。**路边案例**：登山迷路的人被司机趁机要价 1 万英镑。
**2. Zwolinski 的论证**：只适用于工人**自愿**且**充分知情**的情况 → 工人选择血汗工厂，是因为对**他们自己**比其他选项好 → 禁止或抵制 = 替他们做决定、拿走他们的选项（企业会搬走）→ 第三方（政府、抵制团体）不应干预。
⚠️ 区分：“为了国家长远发展可以牺牲现在的工人”**不是** Zwolinski 的论证（那是把工人当手段）。
**3. Kates (2015) 的三个反对**（接受 Zwolinski 的前提，证明结论推不出来，“fails on its own terms”）：① **同意无效**——没有像样的其他选项、缺信息、有时被胁迫，像路边案例；② **待遇底线**——低于底线就是不尊重尊严，哪怕本人愿意；③ **伤害其他工人**——接受恶劣条件削弱了争取更高标准的集体行动。
**4. 企业能做什么**（Vogel 2005）：不一定马上禁止，但企业有义务改善劳工标准；Nike 2002 年开始审核工厂、2005 年发布社会责任报告。
**例子**：Nike；Apple / 富士康成都工厂（Kates 文章开头）；BBC *Undercover: The Refugees Who Make Our Clothes*。
**课件四句总结**：义务论要求尊重基本权利；工人有权选择工作，但……同意并不完全有效，选择也损害别人；全球企业应帮助改善劳工标准。

**难度**：论证集中在 2 篇文章，结构清楚；但要读懂哲学论证（同意、剥削、自主），英文阅读量中等。

## 📖 第 2 题回顾：股东 vs. 利益相关者（第 2 周）
**题目**：Imagine that you are a strategy consultant hired by a large oil company to guide safety standards on oil rigs. Analyse in detail Milton Friedman's Shareholder theory and explain relevant differences with Edward Freeman's stakeholder theory. Then, explain which theory you would advise the oil company to follow, and why?
（假设你是石油公司聘请的战略顾问，负责钻井平台安全标准。详细分析 Friedman 的股东理论，说明它和 Freeman 利益相关者理论的区别，然后说明你会建议公司采用哪种理论、为什么。）
**必读** Friedman (1970)；**推荐** Vogel (2005) 第 1、2、7 章（Moodle 文件名 “Ch. 1.2.7”）。（Freeman 1984、Heath 2014 出自课件）
**必须写到**：义务论 → 股东和 / 或利益相关者理论 → 用推荐阅读讨论对另一理论的反对 → 选哪个、为什么。

**1. Friedman (1970)**：“The social responsibility of business is to increase its profits.” 经理是股东的雇员，要在遵守法律和道德习俗的前提下尽量多赚钱（p. 211）。
**Friedman 的义务论论证**：P1 侵犯他人财产权不允许 → P2 股东拥有公司，有权决定目的 → P3 股东的目的通常是利润 → P4 经理签了合同代表股东 → 结论：经理有受托义务使利润最大化。
可以攻击 P1（为保护别人的权利，如健康权，可以限制财产权）和 P3（股东也可能要长期利润或伦理目标）。
**Friedman 的回应**：平衡各方权利是**政府**的事，经理 ① 没有民主合法性 ② 没有能力 ③ 没有动力；用股东的钱做公益 = “没有代表权的征税”。
**2. Freeman (1984)**：经理要平衡所有利益相关者（员工、客户、供应商、社区、股东）的权利——“不被当作手段”；像所罗门王一样平衡冲突。
**Freeman 的论证**：P1 侵犯任何人权利都不允许 → P2 股东有财产权，别人也有权利 → P3 冲突时要平衡 → P4 政府没做到时经理应该做 → 结论：经理应平衡所有利益相关者。
攻击点在 P4：Friedman 的三条回应；**Heath (2014)**：多重受托义务让经理利用模糊性、逃避问责。
**3. Vogel (2005) 的解决办法**：合法性 → 遵守国际标准、让工人和社区参与；能力 → 请专家；动力 → 市场越来越看重伦理（但不总够）。伦理有时能带来利润，有时不能。
**例子（课上讲得最多）**：**BP 深水地平线漏油**（约 490 万桶、11 人死亡、约 650 亿美元）——赶工省钱、安全不足、过度依赖行业自律；纪录片 60 Minutes：“到底谁说了算”；老师：“有法规是一回事，执法是另一回事”。BlackRock Fink (2019) 的信：“利润和使命不矛盾”。
**有优秀范例**（Moodle 上的样例就是第 2 题），可以看结构。

**难度**：题目给了**顾问角色和具体场景**（钻井安全），容易写出“建议”；BP 案例现成；有范例参考。理论比第 1 题多一个人物，但课上讲得最细。

### 1. 一页总览

### 两份阅读各说了什么

**Friedman (1970)《企业的社会责任就是增加利润》**（4 页）
- 公司经理是股东的**雇员 / 代理人**（agent），责任是按股东意愿经营：在遵守法律和道德习俗（ethical custom）的前提下尽量多赚钱。
- 如果经理拿公司的钱去做“社会责任”，等于**替别人花钱**——相当于未经选举就“征税”和“花税款”，这本该是**政府**的事。
- 经理也不懂怎么解决社会问题（没能力），股东还会把他炒掉（做不长）。
- 但如果某个“社会”开支其实对公司**长期利益**有好处，那就可以做——只是别把它叫作“社会责任”。

**Vogel (2005)《美德的市场》第 1、2、7 章**
- **第 1 章**：90 年代以来企业社会责任（CSR）复兴，主要动力是“**公民规制**”（civil regulation）——消费者、NGO、投资者、员工的压力。CSR 有一个“市场”，但只是**小众策略**（niche），不是所有公司都适用。
- **第 2 章**：“讲伦理能赚钱”（business case）**没有可靠证据**；好公司不一定更赚钱，也不一定更亏钱。很多 CSR 支持者其实已经接受了 Friedman 的前提（公司首要责任是给股东赚钱），只是加了一句“要赚钱就得讲伦理”。
- **第 7 章**：自愿的 CSR 有用，但有结构性上限（成本一大就做不下去）；**光靠市场不够，需要政府的强制规定**。真正负责任的企业还应该**支持提高法定标准**，而不只是自己做得比法律多一点。

### 2. Friedman (1970) 逐页导读

> 你手上的版本是教材 Ghillyer《Business Ethics Now》附录 A 的**重印版**，页码 211–214 印在每页底部。原文登在《纽约时报杂志》1970 年 9 月 13 日。

### p. 211 —— 谁有责任？经理对谁负责？（对应 P2、P4）
- **开头讽刺**：商人们说“企业有社会责任”，以为在保护自由企业，其实是在“宣扬纯粹的社会主义”（preaching pure and unadulterated socialism）。
- **“只有人才有责任”**（Only people have responsibilities）：“企业”作为整体不能有责任，所以要问：具体是**谁**的责任？——公司高管。
- **核心段**：在自由企业、私有财产制度下，**公司高管是企业所有者的雇员**（an employee of the owners of the business），对雇主直接负责；责任是按他们的意愿经营，通常就是“尽可能多赚钱，同时遵守社会的基本规则——法律和道德习俗”。
- 如果公司是为慈善目的成立的（医院、学校），经理的目标就是提供服务，而不是利润。→ 关键不是“利润”本身，而是**经理是所有者的代理人（agent）**。
- 👉 这一页对应课件论证的 **P2（股东拥有公司、决定目的）、P3（目的通常是利润）、P4（经理签合同代表股东）**。
- ⭐ 对钻井安全很重要：Friedman 自己写了“**遵守法律和道德习俗**”这个限制。所以“Friedman = 可以不顾安全”是**误读**。他的立场是：守法 + 守基本道德习俗之内，赚最多的钱。

### p. 212 上半 —— 个人 vs. 经理；“社会责任”到底意味着什么
- 经理**作为个人**可以有很多责任（家庭、良心、教会、国家），可以花**自己的**钱做公益——那时他是“委托人”（principal），不是“代理人”。
- 说经理“作为商人”有社会责任，意思一定是：他要做**不符合雇主利益**的事。Friedman 举了三个例子：不涨价来抑制通胀；**花钱减少污染，超过对公司最有利的程度或超过法律要求**；雇长期失业者而不雇更合格的工人。
- 👉 ⭐ 第二个例子最贴近你的题目：**超过法律要求的安全 / 环保开支**，正是 Friedman 反对的那种“社会责任”（除非它对公司有利）。
- 这样做，经理就是在**花别人的钱**：减少股东回报 = 花股东的钱；涨价 = 花顾客的钱；压工资 = 花员工的钱。

### p. 212 下半 —— 第一条回应：**合法性**（“没有代表权的征税”）
- 经理这样做，等于一边“**征税**”，一边“**决定税款怎么花**”（imposing taxes … and deciding how the tax proceeds shall be spent）。
- 征税和花税款是**政府职能**，有宪法、议会、司法的制衡；美国革命的口号就是“**taxation without representation**”（没有代表权的征税）。
- 经理是股东选的（或自己选自己），却要同时当“**立法者、执行者和法官**”（legislator, executive and jurist）。
- 经理变成了事实上的“公务员”，但不是选出来的 → 在政治原则上“不可容忍”（intolerable）。
- 文中第一次出现 **window-dressing**（装门面）：“只要他们以社会责任名义做的事是真的，而不只是装门面”。
- 结论：“社会责任”学说等于接受社会主义观点——用**政治机制**而不是**市场机制**来分配资源。
- 👉 对应课件里 Friedman 对 Freeman P4 的第一条回应：**经理没有民主合法性**。

### p. 212 末 – p. 213 上 —— 第二条回应：**能力**
- “**How is he to know how to spend it?**”（他怎么知道该怎么花？）（p. 212）
- 经理是经营公司的专家，“但他被选上来，并不能让他成为通胀问题的专家”（p. 213）。他压价到底能不能抑制通胀？还是造成短缺？该让股东、顾客、员工各承担多少成本？
- 👉 对应第二条回应：**经理没有能力**判断社会问题。
- ⭐ 写作提示：Friedman 的“能力”例子是**通胀**这种宏观社会问题。**钻井平台安全**不同——它是公司自己的业务，工程和安全正是经理和公司的专业。这是你可以用来**回应** Friedman 的一个点（见第 6 部分）。

### p. 213 —— 第三条回应：**动力 / 做不长**
- “**Will not the stockholders fire him?**”（股东难道不会炒掉他？）——现在的股东，或者股价跌了以后来收购公司的人。顾客和员工也会转去“不那么讲社会责任”的竞争对手。
- 工会的例子：要求工会领导为“公共目的”限制工资，结果是野猫罢工、基层造反。
- 私营竞争企业的好处：迫使人为自己的行为负责。“**They can do good—but only at their own expense.**”（他们可以做好事，但只能花自己的钱。）
- 👉 对应第三条回应：**经理没有动力 / 做不下去**。

### p. 213 中 —— 预先反驳“政府太慢”
- 有人会说：问题太急，等不及政治程序，企业出手更快。
- Friedman：这在原则上必须拒绝——等于少数人说服不了多数选民，就想用**不民主**的方法达到目的。
- 股东运动（当时的 G.M. 运动）也一样：一部分股东逼另一部分股东出钱。
- **个人业主**不同：他花的是自己的钱，可以做。

### p. 213 下 —— ⭐ “装门面”与长期自利（window-dressing / long-run self-interest）
- “在实践中，社会责任学说常常是一件**外衣**（cloak），掩盖那些其实另有理由的行为。”
- 例子：大雇主给小社区修设施，**可能符合公司长期利益**——更容易招人、降低工资成本、减少偷窃和破坏。还有慈善捐款的税收好处。
- 这类开支“**完全出于公司自身利益**”，顺便赢得好感（goodwill as a by-product）。Friedman **不反对做**，只是反对把它**叫作**“社会责任”——他叫它“**虚伪的装门面**”（hypocritical window-dressing），但说自己“生不起气来”去谴责。
- 👉 ⭐ 对你的论文最重要的一段之一：按 Friedman 的逻辑，如果更高的钻井安全标准能**避免事故、罚款、停产、声誉损失**，那它就是**长期自利**，Friedman 会同意做。所以“Friedman 也会要求安全”这条路是存在的——但理由是**利润**，不是**工人的权利**。这就是两个理论的关键区别。

### p. 214 —— 结尾：市场原则 vs. 政治原则；最著名的那句话
- 商人在自己业务内部很精明，在业务外部却“短视、糊涂”，呼吁价格工资管制、大谈社会责任，最后招来“**政府官僚的铁拳**”（the iron fist of Government bureaucrats）。
- **市场机制的原则是“一致同意”**（unanimity）：没有人能强迫别人，合作都是自愿的。**政治机制的原则是“服从”**（conformity）：少数要服从多数。Friedman 承认有些事避免不了政治机制。
- “社会责任”如果当真，就会把政治机制扩展到所有人类活动。
- **最后一句（最常被引用）**：“企业有且只有一个社会责任——利用资源从事旨在增加利润的活动，**只要它遵守游戏规则**，也就是进行公开、自由的竞争，**没有欺骗或欺诈**。”
- 👉 注意后半句的限制：“**rules of the game**”和“**without deception or fraud**”。写论文时要讲出这个限制，显得你读得仔细。

### 第 7 章 “Beyond the Market for Virtue”（超越美德的市场），pp. 162–173

|---|---|---|

| p. 172 | 支持全行业最低标准的公司，可能比自己减排却反对监管的公司**更有美德**（气候例子）；Hollender：市场力量单独很少足够 | (c) |
| p. 173 | 结尾：“**公民规制和政府规制都有正当作用**”——前者体现美德市场的潜力，后者承认它的局限 | (c) 结论可用 |

- Vogel 自己的结尾句："Civil and government regulation both have a legitimate role to play in improving public welfare." (Vogel, 2005, p. 173)。

> ⚠️ Friedman 第 1 条：重印版写的是 "their basic rules of the society"。有些网上版本写 "the basic rules"。**照你手上 Moodle 版本抄**（their）。

### 6. 写作时的思路线索

> 🔶 **这是帮你看懂的线索，要用你自己的分析和语言写。** 下面只是可能的立场方向和论证材料，不是论文段落；不要照抄。老师要求：**研究没有定论时，选一个答案，然后给理由**——立场一定要明确。

### 先搞清楚两个理论的关键区别（理论部分要讲）

| 比较点 | Friedman（股东理论） | Freeman（利益相关者理论，来自课件） |
|---|---|---|
| 经理对谁负责 | 股东（经理是代理人） p. 211 | 所有利益相关者：员工、顾客、供应商、社区、股东 |
| 义务的来源 | 股东的**财产权** + 合同（P1–P4） | **所有人的权利**，不能被当作手段（P1–P4） |
| 权利冲突时谁来平衡 | **政府**（经理没合法性、能力、动力） pp. 212–213 | 政府没做到时，**经理**来平衡（P4） |
| 安全标准做到哪 | 守法 + 道德习俗；超出法律的开支只有在对公司**长期有利**时才做 pp. 211–213 | 工人和社区的生命健康是**权利**，即使不赚钱也要保护 |
| 和 Vogel 的关系 | Vogel p. 26：今天的经理“几乎只能遵循 Friedman 的格言” | Freeman **战略版**（关注利益相关者能赚钱）≈ Friedman 加一个转折（Vogel p. 26）；**伦理版**才是真正的区别 |

**线索**：钻井安全这题的关键不是“要不要安全”（两个理论都会要求守法），而是“**做到法律最低要求就够了，还是要更高？理由是利润还是权利？**”

### 立场 A：建议采用 Freeman（伦理版）
- **理由线索**：钻井平台事故直接伤害工人生命和沿海社区（课件 BP 案：11 人死亡、渔民收入暴跌）；这是公司**自己的业务**造成的风险，不是 Friedman 说的“一般社会问题”（通胀、贫困）。
- **最强反对**：Friedman 的三条回应 + Heath（课件）——经理没合法性、没能力、没动力；多重受托义务让经理利用模糊性。
- **回应线索**：
  - 能力：Friedman 的例子是通胀（p. 212–213），而安全工程是石油公司的核心专业；Friedman 自己也说商人在“业务内部的事”上很精明（p. 214）。
  - 合法性：课件里 Vogel 的解决办法——遵守国际 / 公共标准、让工人和社区参与决策（Vogel pp. 166–167 讲了政府和国际组织参与的准则）。
  - 模糊性（Heath）：安全标准可以写成清楚、可审计的规则（比如课上的“四眼原则”、事先规定谁有决定权），减少经理钻空子的空间。
  - 动力：Vogel p. 4、p. 165 承认自愿 CSR 有成本上限 → 所以建议要**配合**支持法定标准（p. 171）。

### 立场 B：建议采用 Friedman
- **理由线索**：Friedman 要求守法和道德习俗（p. 211）、不欺骗（p. 214）；安全事故带来巨额罚款和损失（课件 BP 约 650 亿美元），所以高安全标准是**长期自利**（p. 213）；平衡社会利益是政府的事（p. 212）。
- **最强反对**：
  - Vogel：**没有证据**表明讲美德一定赚钱（p. 17），CSR 只是小众策略（p. 3）——如果某个安全投入算下来“不划算”，Friedman 的逻辑就允许不做。
  - 课件 BP 案：政府监管不足、过度依赖行业自律；“有法规是一回事，执法是另一回事”——Friedman 依赖的“游戏规则”在现实里可能很弱（Vogel p. 170：CSR 的效果依赖强有力的公共部门）。
- **回应线索**：可以说重大事故的损失巨大且不可逆，风险管理（Vogel p. 43 引 FT：CSR 是风险管理）让高安全标准几乎总在公司长期利益之内；而且 Friedman 的框架下，公司可以支持**更严的法律**，让所有竞争者遵守同一标准（这一点要小心：Friedman p. 214 批评商人呼吁政府管制，你得自己论证为什么安全规则不同于价格管制）。
- ⚠️ 难点：选 Friedman 时，你要正面回答“如果安全措施不赚钱怎么办”，否则老师会觉得论证有漏洞。

### 立场 C：有条件的立场（例如“以 Freeman 为原则，用 Vogel 的现实限制来设计”）
- **理由线索**：Freeman 的权利理由决定“为什么要做”；Vogel 第 7 章决定“怎么做才可持续”——公司自己提高标准（second-best, p. 163），同时支持行业 / 法定最低标准（p. 171–172），避免“单独行动撑不久”（p. 166）。
- **最强反对**：“骑墙”——老师要求明确立场；或者 Heath 的模糊性问题。
- **回应线索**：开头和结论**明确写出你建议的是哪一个理论**（例如“我建议采用利益相关者理论”），Vogel 只是用来说明如何落地；不要写成“两个都对”。

### 反对意见的三种类型（论文指导课件）
- **内部反对**：Friedman vs. Freeman 都是义务论，互相反驳（P4 之争）。
- **经验反对**：Vogel 第 2 章——讲伦理不一定赚钱、因果方向不清。
- **外部反对**：从后果主义角度（第 3 周才讲；用的话要注意“只能用本周阅读”的规则，先问老师）。
